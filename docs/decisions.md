# Decisions

Why the language is the way it is, a row for each decision, and what the July specs have that isn't built.
`language.md` is the reference for what exists. Add a row here when the language changes.

## The language

Newest first. "Why" is the reason at the time, so these can be revisited.

| Date | Decision | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-11-26 | A param's caption is a string after its name, before its default or its range: `param q "arms" = 8 in 3..16 step 1`, and for a choice `param along "direction" in {1, i, 1 + i}` or `param along "direction" = "i" in {…}`. It's words: one with `{…}` in it is an error that says a value is a readout's, one with `$…$` is an error that says to write the symbol, and an empty one is an error. Without one a control says the param's name, set as mathematics | Kris's sketch on issue 4, and where a beat writes its caption, `beat spin "caption"`, so a figure's short words sit beside what they name. A string after a param's name was a parse error, so nothing that compiled changes, and the corpus's IR is the same but for the two figures that now have captions. Before the name it would read as the name. Not values: what a control says doesn't change as the figure plays, and a value that does is what a readout is for. Not mathematics yet: a control's label is the page's text, and setting mathematics there needs the core's layout as a readout has it, which no figure has asked for. A Unicode symbol, `"θ"`, is words |
| 2026-11-26 | A choice of four options or fewer is radios, and of five or more a `<select>`. A choice of two options written `0` and `1`, or named `off` and `on`, is a checkbox, checked for the second. A range is a slider. The core works the kind out, from these and the sheets, and says it in `info.params[].control`, so the element, a page's build and the playground agree | Kris prefers radios for a small set, since every option shows and is one key away, and a `<select>` hides them. Four fits a row at a phone's width with short options, and bounds' two cases become radios. Off and on is how `TriangleLayers` writes its layers (`migration.md`), and the blog draws those as checkboxes. Two options that are something else, `{"plain": 0, "lit": 1}`, say what each is, so they stay radios. Not chosen: deciding in the element, which would leave the playground and a build to agree with it by copy |
| 2026-11-26 | A sheet reaches a param's control as a thing whose type is `control` and whose name is its param's: `style control { … }` for every control and `style q.control { … }` for `q`'s, or a choice's by the name its value has, `along.control`. It takes `text`, the parts a label's `text` takes, `caption = value` by default, with the value said after a slider, and `kind`, which is `slider`, `radios`, `select` or `checkbox`. A rule for every control sets a kind only where it fits, a rule that names one has to fit it, and a name that isn't a param's is an error. `style q` alone still names a mark, and is an error with no mark `q`. So `control` is reserved: a name or a tag `control` anywhere in a figure is an error that says it's a control's type | The language's own pattern: a mark's type is one of its tags, so `circle.blue` and `c.aux` read the same way, and the control of `q` is `q.control`, declared, with nothing reached by a rule written for something else. Not `q::control`: `::` is a side of a mark, and a control isn't one. Not `style q` for both the label and the control, as a dot and its label share a rule: a sheet that styles `q`'s label would change its control's words without saying so. A rule for every control can't know which are choices, so it applies where it can, as a rule by type may match nothing. Reserved rather than shared, from a review: `style control { fill: red }` for a mark called `control` had become an error about a control's properties, and a word that means two things in one selector would need a rule to say which. No figure in the corpus or the labs uses it |
| 2026-11-26 | A label written without `at` is a readout: it isn't drawn in the figure, and a page says it as text beside it, after the controls. A screen reader is given its mathematics in words, "z squared = 1". It's written among the figure's own statements, and one in a group or a panel is an error. One that doesn't exist this frame says `—`, and a label's own value now counts toward whether it exists, as its place and its text's values did. `offset` or `away` without `at` is an error. A readout is laid out by the core, in ems, as a label's text is, and the page sets it in the label fonts | Issue 4, decided by Kris: a readout is a label with no place. Before this, `label n = "…"` without `at` was a parse error, expected `at`, in a figure with panels too, so no figure meant anything by it and none changed. It says which value it is by default, where a label says its value alone, because a label's point says which value it is and beside the figure nothing does. From a review: `"{q - 1}"` first said only `2`. The spoken form is read from the same reading the layout uses, so it can't say what isn't drawn. Not in a group, which can be drawn more than once and through a map, or a panel, since a readout is said once for the whole figure, and whether one can be placed in a panel is still open. A label whose own value didn't exist used to stop the frame. The core's layout means `$…$` reads the same beside the figure as over it |
| 2026-11-26 | A readout's box is as wide and as tall as the most it says over the moments a reader can reach, which are tried: the poster's, and the one a build or a page starts the figure at, with the params it holds (`--param` and `--t`, the Astro component's `poster`, the element's `params` and time); 33 through the clock from there; each param from there at every step if it has fewer than 16, and at nine places along its range otherwise; and every param at its start or its end together. From a review: a param without a step was tried only at its ends, often whole numbers with no decimals, so `t² = {t·t}` at t = 0.37 ran 19.6 px past its box and showed `t² = 0`, and a build asked to start elsewhere wasn't tried where it started. | 2026-11-25 | An implicit curve's trace is kept within a quarter of a device pixel of the curve both ways: every point traced is near the curve, and every point of the curve in view is near the trace. Each cell's line is checked against the curve's direction where it crosses the cell's edges, and against the field's sign a quarter of a pixel either side of its middle. Where the two sides agree with something else between them as well, a pole, a jump, the edge of where the field has a value or another part of the curve, Newton steps from the middle decide it: they settle only on a zero, and the line is near where they settle within a quarter of a pixel. A line that strays is bent onto the curve, halved at the curve until every piece is near it, without leaving its cell by more than two of the finest cells, so no two cells' lines follow one piece of the curve. A bend that didn't settle is kept only where its points go on from one end to the other in order. One that won't bend, because it runs from one part of the curve to another or a tip reaches past its cell, splits its cell in four, down to a sixteenth of a cell, 0.375 pixels, and each neighbour whose side gains a corner is looked at again, so splitting follows two arms, or a tip, from cell to cell. Where a part has more than two crossings and can't be split, they're paired by the field's sign at its middle, which is a guess, and its lines are kept straight. A crossing whose Newton steps leave its edge, land further from where the edge changes sign than they started, or go further than the last, as across a cube root's vertical tangent, is found along the edge instead, between the two points the edge was found to change sign between, by Newton steps that halve where they'd leave, and by halving alone, without the gradient, to a millionth of a cell, once two have left. Steps that shrink by a steady ratio, as at a squared or cubed field's zero, are taken as many times over as that says. A loop starts where it crosses the horizontal through the centroid of the area it encloses, worked out from the mean of its points. The work is bounded for each trace: checking, bending and splitting work the field out about 12 times for each corner of the grid, and half as much again at most for the parts splits have made, which are looked at after. Coarse cells come first, and the furthest astray first among them. Only splitting holds budget back for the parts it makes; a line is checked and bent while any is left. Past that a line is left as the grid found it, with nothing to tell the reader. The grid's own work isn't counted: its corners, whether each edge that changes sign goes to zero, and putting its crossings on the curve | Issue 31: `sin(30·x) = y` at 1633 pixels had each peak's stroke cut flat at a different height, and the fill, which is the field's sign at each pixel, showed past it. A peak turns in under half a pixel, and the grid joined its two sides across a cell below the tip. The same cut thin loops short with an island beyond them (`sin(x + y) - cos(x·y) + 1 = 0` in `0 ± 10`, from a comparison with Desmos), joined two circles 0.003 apart into one, and capped spikes whose arms never meet. A quarter of a pixel is a quarter of a pixel's coverage at most under a box filter, so even a 1 px stroke covers the fill's edge, and the tests measure 0.25 at most both ways, against dense samples worked out without the tracer, at several sizes and grid positions. Bending first is the cheap repair where one arc is only too curved, as at most tips of `sin(30·x)`, and splitting is for what bending can't tell apart. From two reviews of the first versions: a loop's start followed the mean of its points, which bending moved, and then the centroid summed far from the origin, so a circle at (10⁶, 10⁶) started anywhere from −166° to −178°; a field whose gradient vanishes on the curve, as `(x² + y² - r²)·abs(x² + y² - r²)` does, looked astray everywhere to a check against the gradient, and cost six to ten times what it had, where it now costs what it did (14.9 ms at 1633 pixels, 14.2 on main); the check by sign took a line beside a pole, a cusp or the edge of a square root for one that strays, and bent it to 256 points, as the astroid's 341 became 6509; bends reaching 4 cells out traced pieces of `sin(100·x)·sin(100·y) = 0` that other cells traced as well; the budget went to the rows the grid reached first; and a line could go unchecked while budget was held back for parts not yet looked at. From a third review: where a part's crossings were paired by a guess and bent, two lines followed one piece of `sin(x + y) - cos(x·y) + 1 = 0` at 375 × 812; the check by sign also sampled a sixteenth of a pixel apart and checked that the field had fallen a thousandfold where Newton steps settled, and neither decided anything, since the steps settle only on a zero; and a field that falls as a cube root, `sign(y - sin(30·x))·abs(y - sin(30·x))^(1/3) = 0`, cost six times what it did on main at 812 pixels, 60 ms against 9.7, where Newton steps went on twelve times, each twice as far as the last, and the search along each edge worked out the gradient at every halving. It now costs 17.6 ms, where each of main's three Newton steps took a crossing further off the curve, and works the field out 2.1 times as often for each crossing as `sin(30·x) = y` does. Issue 29: Newton steps had left points off the curve where they don't settle, 0.015 off `y = sign(x)·abs(x)^(1/3)` at 47 cells across and 7.6 × 10⁵ off `tanh(50·(x² + y² - 1)) = 0`, and both are now on it to within 3 × 10⁻¹¹. `self.dist` measures to the better trace as well. Every corpus render is unchanged to the byte, and at 1280 × 720 the corpus circle's trace takes 3.4 ms as it did, and `sin(30·x) = y` 7.4 ms where it took 5.2. The budget of 12 is a trade. It reaches every curve the tests trace within a quarter of a pixel, among them `sin(80·x) = y` at 812 pixels, which needs 6.7, the close arms of `1/sin(x)² = y + 2` in `0 ± 51` at 812, which need 8.4, and a curve made at random at 375, which needs 8.5, where 8 left those last two astray by a pixel and a half and by half a pixel. Each unit costs a figure that strays everywhere about a fifth of what its grid does, so past what the grid can follow a trace costs about three times what it did (`sin(x + y) - cos(x·y) + 1 = 0` in `0 ± 40` at 1100 pixels, 134 ms where it took 41), where 8 would cost 111 and 16 would cost 159. Not chosen: interval arithmetic, which would find what no corner sees but needs bounds for every built-in. Its limits: a loop smaller than a cell that no corner or edge sees isn't found, nor a spike that falls between corners at every row, and two arms closer than about 0.4 pixels are joined. Near a pole or a jump the curve still stops within a cell or so of it, as before. A curve with more double points than the budget reaches has its crossings cut by up to a pixel, as the 1600 of `sin(30·x)·sin(30·y) = 0` at 812 pixels are, which would need 33. Where the budget runs out, which cells it reached depends on how far their lines strayed, and cells near that cut can change from one frame to the next as a figure moves, so such a figure can flicker there. Desmos says when it can't resolve a curve. The trace knows when its budget ran out, so a figure could too, and doesn't yet |

| 2026-11-25 | An implicit curve is traced only where its expression goes to zero. Where the expression changes sign across a pole instead, as `y = tan(x)` does at x = ±π/2 and `y = 1/x` at 0, or across a jump, as `atan2(y, x)` does along its cut and `floor(x)` at each whole number, nothing is traced, and the curve stops at the edge of the grid cell the pole or jump runs through. At a zero the expression falls toward 0, about 2¹²-fold in twelve halvings where it's smooth, and at a pole it only grows, so the expression says where its poles are and the author doesn't have to. The distance estimated as f over the length of its gradient can't tell them apart, since it goes to 0 at a pole too, which is why Newton steps had pulled the false line toward the asymptote. A jump fails the same test, unless it's smaller than the expression changes across about a cell. It's the tracer's counterpart to a parametric curve not joining samples across a jump (2026-11-06). Only edges that change sign pay: six evaluations more for most zeros, twelve at a pole, eighteen at most. On the corpus circle at 1280 × 720 a trace takes 15% longer, half a millisecond.
